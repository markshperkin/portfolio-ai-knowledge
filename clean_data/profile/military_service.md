# Military Service

## Summary
I served 3 years in the **Israeli Air Force (IAF)** — mandatory service for Israeli citizens. I held **Elite Athlete status** for my entire service because I was competing as a national-level swimmer at the time, which meant my service and my training had to coexist. The biggest professional carry-over from those years is **discipline, accountability, and learning how to talk to people** to get what you need without burning trust.

## Context
Every Israeli citizen serves. I served as required, and I served while simultaneously doing competitive swimming at a national level — which means the IAF wasn't just military service for me, it was military service *on top of* a full-time elite training schedule. Both jobs, every day, for three years.

## Role
- **Branch:** Israeli Air Force (IAF)
- **Duration:** 3 years (mandatory service)
- **Status:** Elite Athlete — granted because of competitive swimming
- **Locations:** Served in two locations — deliberately not specifying.

## The Reality of Serving as an Elite Athlete
Training was a full-time job. Military service was also a full-time job. Both at the same time, for three years straight. That meant:
- Some practices I missed because of military obligations.
- Some days I got approval to skip the base because of a competition or a training camp.
- Constant negotiation between two demanding institutions, neither of which existed to accommodate the other.

## Biggest Lesson — Talking to People
The single biggest skill I took out of the IAF wasn't military — it was **communication and credibility management**.

I had to convince officers repeatedly that I had to leave early for practice, *without coming across as a slacker who was using swimming as an excuse*. I wasn't slacking. But the perception risk was real every single time — and protecting that perception while still getting what I needed was its own skill.

That's a workplace skill, not a military one. I use it now: how do you advocate for what you need, in a system that doesn't naturally give it to you, without losing the trust of the people around you?

## Professional Carry-Over
- **Discipline** — two full-time jobs at the same time for three years builds a baseline that's hard to break.
- **Accountability** — military culture is the opposite of "it wasn't my fault." Things are owned.
- **Communication under hierarchy** — talking to officers about non-standard requests taught me how to manage upward, respectfully and effectively.

## What I'd Have Done If Not for Swimming
I'll be honest — swimming took most of my service away from me. I wish I'd had the chance to do more. If I hadn't been on the athlete track, I'd have gone for **Shayetet 13** (Israeli naval special forces) or **Unit 8200** (signals intelligence / tech). Those would have been the targets.

That's not a regret exactly — swimming was the right choice for the trajectory I was on, and the years served their purpose. But it's an honest acknowledgement that the elite-athlete carve-out limited what kind of military experience I actually got.

## Lessons Learned
The IAF years are part of why I started university at 21 instead of 18 — more mature, more focused, used to operating under load. And the communication skill from negotiating with officers is something I still actively use in engineering contexts: when you need a deviation from the default and you have to protect your credibility while asking for it, you've done this before.
