# Personality and Values

## Summary
I'm direct but with manners and patience, communication-obsessed, and a natural driver — the person who takes the wheel on a long road trip without thinking twice. I commit 100% once I've picked a path, I love being around people who push themselves the way I do, and the thing that actually energizes me is creating something out of nothing. This file is the day-to-day "what am I like to work with" version of me, separate from the athlete-mentality framing.

## Context
There's an interview-positioning version of me in `what_sets_him_apart.md` that leads with the 20-year athlete story. That's accurate but it's the highlight reel. This file is the more granular picture: how I actually communicate, how I make decisions, what I value in teammates, and the moments that genuinely light me up.

## How I Communicate
- **Direct, with manners and patience.** I'll tell you what I think, but I won't run someone over to do it.
- **Communication is the most important thing in any workplace** — not the code, not the architecture, the communication. I believe this strongly.
- **I ask a lot of questions.** Two reasons: I want to learn as much as I can, and I want to nail the requirements. Understanding what's actually being asked is the crucial part of doing the job well. (See also: why I want to be a Forward Deployed Engineer — second-hand requirements taught me this lesson the hard way.)

## How I Make Decisions
My actual workflow when I hit a fork:
1. **Pros and cons** — work through it on paper, get the tradeoffs clear.
2. **Consult a trusted friend** — ideally someone technical in the relevant field. Lay out the options, hear their read.
3. **Reflect, sleep on it** — give it a day if I can.
4. **Execute** — and once I'm executing, I'm all in.

The 100% rule matters: when I pick a path, I put everything into it. That way if it doesn't work out, I know for a fact I did my absolute best. **No regrets** — and if I fail, I *learn* and next time I choose differently. The best lessons come from failures.

## What Energizes Me
The thing that makes me lose track of time isn't a specific tech stack — it's the act of **creating something out of nothing**. A quote that resonated with me captures it:

> "Once in a life, a person touched a computer and told it what to do, and the computer did it. And then the person felt like god — a very small god, in that small environment he created to himself."

That's how I feel when I code. Every problem is doomed to be solved. Every challenge is meant to be overcome, and when I overcome it, I feel like that one small god, who can create something out of nothing.

## What I'm Like Day-to-Day
- I do most of my work alone, but **I'm easy to be around.** I've never walked into a new place and ended up in conflict with someone, or had someone not like me. That's not an accident — it's the manners-and-patience piece doing its job.
- **I like to chat and laugh while putting in the work.** Light environment, serious output.
- **I enjoy collaboration and solo deep work both.** Pick the right mode for the right problem.

## The Driver Anecdote
A story that captures how I default in groups: at USC, our swim team had a meeting with our team psychologist. She asked: *"If you and your friends had a 10-hour drive, raise your hand if you'd rather drive or sit in the passenger seat."* Everyone raised their hand for passenger. I was the only one who picked driver.

She asked me why. I said: I'd rather drive and get us there safe — I know I can do a good job at it, and I should do it. If everyone else wants to be passenger princesses and do nothing, I'd rather lead the team and drive results.

That's me by default. Not loud-leadership, just *take-the-wheel* leadership.

## What I Value in Teammates
- **Same mindset.** I don't want to work with people who clock in to punch a card. I want teammates who give as much effort as I do and are passionate about the work.
- **I don't mind carrying the team** — I'll do extra. But the team succeeds *much* more when everyone is doing the same.

A quote I keep in my head:

> "If you surround yourself with successful people, the chances you succeed increase by 30%. But if you surround yourself with unsuccessful people, the chances you succeed drop by 60%."

That asymmetry is real. Who you work with matters more than most people admit.

## Lessons Learned
The communication piece is the one I'd underline. Skill gets you considered, but if you can't get the requirements straight and can't talk to people, the rest doesn't matter. Everything else — the decision framework, the 100% commitment, the leader-by-default — those are habits I can rely on under pressure because I've been running them for 20+ years.
