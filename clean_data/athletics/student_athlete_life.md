# Life as a USC Student-Athlete

## Summary
For 5 seasons at USC I lived the full Division I student-athlete schedule — two practices a day, gym, recovery, and a CS degree on top of it. I also took on a self-initiated leadership role with the team that no one assigned to me.

## Context
I came to USC at 21, after military service, older than most of my teammates. I was a Division I SEC swimmer and a Computer Science student at the same time, every single day of the competitive season, for five years.

## Technical Details — The Daily Schedule
This repeated every day of the competitive season:
- 4:45am — wake up
- 5:45am — at the pool
- 6:00–8:00am — morning practice (in water)
- 8:00–9:45am — gym
- 10:30am–1/2pm — classes
- 2:30pm — back at the pool
- 3:00–5:00pm — afternoon practice (in water)
- Evening — dinner, homework, recovery
- Weekly — nutrition management, physical therapy, massage, sleep discipline
- Every other weekend — dual meets, full team travel
- SEC week — a full week away from campus, with all coursework finished before leaving

Total athletic commitment was 30+ hours a week on top of a demanding CS curriculum.

## Team Culture
The team was tight-knit, almost family-like. There was real internal competition at practices and dual meets, but it sat alongside genuine support. The ethos was simple: work hard and support your teammates.

## Leadership Role (self-initiated)
For all 5 seasons I led **pre-SEC confidence meetings** — 30-minute presentations I designed and delivered myself on performing under pressure at big meets. I also ran **one-on-one sessions** with individual swimmers during high-stress periods.

Nobody on the coaching staff asked me to do this. I took it on because it felt natural given my age (older than most teammates), my experience, and a gap I saw that wasn't being filled. Going into the most important meet of the year, swimmers looked to me for that leadership. This is the clearest example I have of leading without a title — leading because it was needed, not because it was assigned.

## Challenges
The hardest part wasn't the training itself — it was sustaining quality academic work while physically exhausted, with zero margin for wasted time. There was no slack in the schedule to recover from a bad day.

## Results
Five seasons of SEC scoring, a CS degree earned alongside it, multiple Dean's List semesters, and a leadership role I built myself that the team relied on at the highest-stakes moment of every season.

## Lessons Learned
Time management isn't a productivity trick — it's a survival skill when two full-time commitments overlap. And leadership doesn't require a title; if you see a gap and you have the experience to fill it, fill it.
