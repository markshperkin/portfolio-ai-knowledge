# The Olympic Near-Miss and the Retirement Decision

## Summary
In 2024 I was 35 hundredths of a second away from a realistic shot at Israel's Paris Olympics relay team, in the best shape of my life. I chose the M.S. in AI instead and retired from competitive swimming at 26. I do not regret it.

## Context
I graduated USC in May 2024. My 2023 personal best in the 100m freestyle was 49.85 (long course). To make Israel's Paris 2024 relay, the time I needed was 49.50 — 35 hundredths away. I was in the best physical condition of my entire career. But I was no longer a student-athlete with built-in infrastructure — the pool, the coaching, the schedule, the funding all came with being a USC athlete, and that was ending.

## The Decision
This wasn't an easy call and I want to be honest about how I weighed it.

**What the Olympics offered:** a lifelong dream. But realistically, the value was largely symbolic — the title of Olympian, the Olympic rings tattoo. Enormous personally, but symbolic.

**What the M.S. in AI offered:** a career I genuinely love, that challenges me continuously, and that lets me provide for a family.

**The role of my relationship:** during the USC years, swimming left almost no time for my relationship with my girlfriend (now my wife). The accumulated sacrifice — on both sides — was real. I wanted to propose and start a family, and chasing elite swimming was incompatible with the life I actually wanted to build.

I chose the M.S. I retired at 26.

## Results
I traded the world's biggest athletic stage for the stage of software engineering and AI. I made that trade deliberately and consciously — not by drifting out of the sport, but by sitting down, weighing it, and deciding.

## Lessons Learned
The line I keep coming back to: *"After making that decision, I do not regret it."* That's the whole "zero regrets" philosophy in one decision. I put 100% into the swimming, so when I walked away I knew exactly what I was walking away from and exactly why. No second-guessing. The same framework — be honest about what each path is really worth, decide, and own it — is how I approach career decisions now.
