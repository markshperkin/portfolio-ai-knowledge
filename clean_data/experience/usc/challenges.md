# USC (TA) — Challenges

## Summary
The hard parts of the TA role: balancing 20 hours/week of TA work against full-time 700-level graduate coursework, handling academic-integrity violations, IDE-compatibility edge cases in the grading pipeline, teaching reinforcement learning (consistently the hardest topic for students), and the underlying financial pressure of needing the role to fund the degree.

## 1. Time Management: TA + Full-Time Graduate Student
20 hours/week as sole TA across multiple courses, simultaneously carrying 12–13 credits of 700-level graduate coursework. No bandwidth for inefficiency. **Resolution:** built the automated grading pipeline to reclaim 10–18 hours/week of otherwise-manual grading. Treated time as a constrained resource and engineered around the bottleneck.

## 2. Academic Integrity Violations
I caught students cheating on assignments. Reporting felt ethically uncomfortable — I was aware of the consequences for the students involved. **Resolution:** I reported the violations. The rationale: not reporting would be unfair to the students who did the work honestly. Followed protocol despite the personal discomfort. (Not going into further specifics.)

## 3. IDE Compatibility Issues in the Grading Pipeline
Some students developed in VS Code, others in Eclipse, which occasionally caused submission errors the automated scripts couldn't handle cleanly. **Resolution:** manual review for affected submissions — only 1–2 cases per grading cycle, an edge case rather than a systemic problem.

## 4. Reinforcement Learning Is Hard to Teach
RL concepts (on-policy vs. off-policy, game theory) were consistently the hardest for students to internalize — abstract, without clear analogies. **Resolution:** whiteboard-based verbal explanations focused on trade-offs and intuition rather than math. The course was breadth-first so deep technical dives weren't the goal, but getting students to understand *when and why* RL applies took repeated effort.

## 5. Financial Pressure
As an international student, USC tuition was too expensive to manage without income. The TA position wasn't just a professional opportunity — it was financially necessary to stay enrolled. **Context:** this created underlying pressure throughout the role; performing well as a TA was tied directly to my ability to continue the degree.

## Lessons Learned
Two things: engineer around your worst time sink before it sinks you (the grading pipeline existed because manual grading would have broken the schedule), and do the right thing even when it's uncomfortable (reporting integrity violations to be fair to honest students).
